# What Spring Training Taught Us About ABS

**Desk:** The Weekly Rotation  
**Byline:** Colby Reichenbach  
**Status:** Launch feature draft  
**Data basis:** 2026 MLB Spring Training ABS games from February 20, 2026 through March 24, 2026. Final dataset includes 451 final spring games and 1,913 tracked ABS challenges.

**Dek:** MLB's first full spring of challenge-era ABS showed real team separation, real umpire variance, and early evidence that challenge timing may matter more than raw volume.

Spring was supposed to introduce ABS. Instead, it gave us the first usable sample of how clubs might actually live with it.

Across 451 final Spring Training games, MLB clubs used the ABS challenge system 1,913 times. The overall overturn rate settled at 52.9 percent. More important, 443 of those 451 games, just over 98 percent, featured at least one challenge. That is enough usage to stop treating ABS as a novelty. By the final week of camp, the challenge system was showing up in normal game flow, and once that happened the more interesting question was no longer whether teams would use ABS. It was how they would use it.

My read from the spring is straightforward: the first meaningful ABS sample already suggests that clubs are separating into different operating styles. Some teams challenged often. Some challenged early. Some held more of their challenge traffic for later innings. And the data hints that the most interesting distinction may not be raw volume at all, but what kind of challenge environment a club is willing to enter.

That does not mean spring solved ABS. It means the sample is large enough to show directional behavior, even if it is still too small to make permanent claims about team quality, organizational superiority, or umpire talent.

## Spring Produced Real ABS Identities

At the league level, spring was not tentative.

- 451 final spring games
- 1,913 tracked challenges
- 1,012 overturned calls
- 52.9 percent overall overturn rate
- 4.24 challenges per game
- 443 of 451 games with at least one challenge

That volume matters because a system used in more than 98 percent of games stops being hypothetical. It becomes part of the baseball environment. Once that happens, differences in team behavior become meaningful.

The first clear split was simple challenge volume. The Yankees led the spring with 101 challenges. The Twins followed with 89, then the Rockies with 84, the Guardians with 78, and the White Sox with 77. On the lower end of the spring sample, the Tigers finished with 40, the Diamondbacks with 44, and both the Mets and Dodgers with 47.

That alone does not prove anything strategic. More aggressive usage could reflect process, roster mix, spring experimentation, or simple noise. But it does establish that clubs were not approaching ABS uniformly. Some treated it like a regular part of their decision surface almost immediately. Others were materially more selective.

The second split is more revealing. Teams were not just separating by how often they challenged. They were separating by when those challenges appeared.

Among clubs with at least 40 spring challenges, the Twins used 46.1 percent of their challenges in innings one through three. The Angels were at 45.9 percent. The White Sox were at 45.5 percent. That profile suggests early engagement: clubs willing to deploy the system quickly rather than preserving it for the back third of the game.

Other clubs looked different. The Cubs used 38.6 percent of their challenges in the seventh inning or later, the highest late-game share in the 40-plus-challenge group. The Cardinals were at 34.4 percent. The Diamondbacks were at 36.4 percent, although on a smaller total of 44 challenges. Those are not identical teams, but the spring did show the outline of a different posture: more willingness to let challenge usage migrate later.

That matters because once ABS becomes routine, volume is only the surface layer. The stronger analytical question is whether clubs are treating challenge opportunities as a generic correction mechanism or as a managed resource with timing attached to it.

## Timing Appears More Revealing Than Raw Volume

The strongest spring finding was not that challenges were overturned 52.9 percent of the time. It was that the challenge environment changed materially across game states.

Challenges in innings one through three were overturned 58.8 percent of the time. In innings four through six, that rate dropped to 52.8 percent. In the seventh inning or later, but not in close games, the overturn rate fell to 47.0 percent. In truly late-and-close spots, seventh inning or later with the score within one run, the overturn rate dropped again to 40.3 percent.

That gap is too large to ignore. It is also the place where the spring dataset starts to become analytically useful.

A lower overturn rate in late-and-close situations does not automatically mean clubs were making worse decisions. It may mean those decisions were being made in a harsher environment: tighter margins, fewer obvious misses, more incentive to challenge marginal calls, and a scarcer challenge resource. In other words, challenge quality may not be declining as much as challenge difficulty is rising.

That distinction matters if the goal is to understand what ABS may look like in the regular season. Teams do not play most consequential games under spring conditions, but spring did show that the challenge mix changes once leverage rises. That means we should be careful about treating overall overturn rate as the main measure of team ABS quality.

The Cardinals are the cleanest example of why timing deserves its own treatment. They finished with 61 challenges, a 65.6 percent overturn rate, and the highest late-and-close share among the 40-plus-challenge teams at 16.4 percent. That does not prove that St. Louis has solved the system. The sample is too small for that. But it does show a profile worth monitoring: above-average volume, strong conversion, and meaningful willingness to challenge in the part of the game where overturns are generally harder to find.

The Cubs offer a slightly different version of the same idea. Their 70 challenges came with the highest late-game share in the 40-plus group, but a lower late-and-close share than St. Louis. That suggests one of the better spring examples of a club whose timing profile may matter more than its raw total.

## The Sample Is Big Enough To Matter, But Still Small Enough To Mislead

This is where the spring needs to be handled carefully.

The league sample is meaningful. Four hundred fifty-one final games and 1,913 challenges are enough to say that ABS was heavily used and that team behavior was not flat. But the moment the analysis moves into team or umpire splits, the sample gets thinner fast.

Even within the 40-plus-challenge team group, the spread only runs from 40 to 101 challenges. That is enough to spot tendencies. It is not enough to declare permanent truths about which organizations are smartest, which dugouts are best prepared, or which clubs have already optimized challenge strategy.

The same caution applies to umpire analysis. Jen Pawol and Mitch Trzeciak each saw 47 challenged calls, Pete Talkington saw 40, and Matt Blackborow and Derek Thomas each saw 39. That is real exposure, and the resulting overturn range was wide enough to notice. Among umpires with at least 25 challenged calls, Charlie Welling finished at 64.7 percent overturned, Matt Blackborow at 64.1 percent, Pete Talkington at 62.5 percent, and Jen Pawol at 61.7 percent. On the low end of that sample floor, Macon Hammond finished at 36.0 percent and Marvin Hudson at 38.5 percent.

That is not noise-free, but it is also not stable enough to turn into hard reputational claims. The right interpretation is narrower: ABS pressure was not evenly distributed, and some plate environments were clearly producing different review outcomes than others.

That should matter to clubs. ABS is not experienced in the abstract. It is experienced against particular catcher setups, hitter profiles, dugout habits, and umpire environments. Spring did not settle those variables, but it did show that they are interacting already.

## What This May Tell Us About the Regular Season

The most responsible spring conclusion is not that one team already "gets" ABS and another does not. It is that some clubs appear to have entered the system with different priors.

Some of those differences may reflect organizational preparation. Some may reflect who was empowered to challenge. Some may reflect different tolerance for early-game challenge usage versus late-game conservation. Some may simply reflect spring noise that disappears by mid-April.

But if the broad profiles hold, and that is the key conditional, the regular season may reveal that clubs are making different bets about ABS:

- whether to use it aggressively as a correction tool
- whether to hold it for higher-pressure game states
- whether a modest overall overturn rate can still be valuable if it is concentrated later
- whether some dugouts are entering games with a clearer idea of what kinds of misses they are willing to contest

That is the regular-season question I care about most. Not who won spring overturn rate, but whether the spring profiles were the first evidence of actual organizational philosophy.

Opening Day will not answer that alone. But it will tell us quickly whether the spring patterns were mostly noise or the beginning of something more durable. If the Yankees remain a high-volume club, if the Twins and Angels stay front-loaded, and if the Cardinals or Cubs continue to skew later, then spring will start to look less like experimentation and more like the first sketch of real ABS strategy.

Spring did not solve the system. It did something more useful. It gave ABS its first visible shape.
