# What Spring Training Taught Us About ABS

**Desk:** The Weekly Rotation  
**Byline:** Colby Reichenbach  
**Status:** Launch feature draft  
**Data basis:** 2026 MLB Spring Training ABS games from February 20, 2026 through March 24, 2026. Final dataset includes 451 final spring games and 1,913 tracked ABS challenges.

**Dek:** MLB's first full spring of challenge-era ABS showed real team identities, real umpire variance, and early evidence that challenge timing may matter more than raw volume.

Spring was supposed to be the introduction. By the end, it looked more like a stress test.

Across 451 final Spring Training games, clubs used the ABS challenge system 1,913 times. The overall overturn rate settled at 52.9 percent, and 443 of those 451 games, just over 98 percent, featured at least one challenge. That matters because it means ABS did not spend the spring living on the margins. Teams used it constantly, umpires were exposed to it every day, and challenge behavior stopped looking like novelty almost immediately.

That is the first real lesson from the spring: ABS is already producing identifiable baseball behavior. Teams were not all using the system the same way. Some clubs challenged aggressively. Some clubs were more front-loaded. Some clubs leaned later into games. And if you only look at overturn rate, you miss the more interesting part of the spring entirely, which is that timing and game state already appear to shape challenge behavior as much as raw correctness.

## Spring Was the First Real Stress Test

At the league level, the spring data looked busy, not tentative.

- 451 final spring games
- 1,913 total challenges
- 1,012 overturned calls
- 52.9 percent overall overturn rate
- 4.24 challenges per game
- 443 of 451 games with at least one challenge

That last number is probably the cleanest indicator that ABS moved quickly from experiment to routine. A system that shows up in more than 98 percent of games is no longer a sideshow. It becomes part of the daily structure of the game, and that means the interesting questions shift quickly. The question is not whether teams will use ABS. The question is how they will use it, when they will use it, and whether some clubs are already using the challenge economy better than others.

## Teams Already Started Separating

The spring leaderboard was not flat.

The Yankees finished with 101 tracked challenges, the highest spring total in the dataset. The Twins followed at 89, then the Rockies at 84, the Guardians at 78, and the White Sox at 77. On the low-volume end, the Tigers finished at 40, with the Diamondbacks at 44 and both the Mets and Dodgers at 47.

That range matters. Every club used the system, but not every club used it at the same rate. Even in a limited spring window, teams were already separating into recognizable challenge identities.

Some looked volume-oriented. The Yankees, Twins, Rockies, and Guardians spent the spring challenging often enough that ABS looked embedded in their normal game flow. Other clubs were clearly more selective. The Tigers still used ABS, but their 40 challenges were less than half of the Yankees' total.

The more interesting split, though, is not just between aggressive and selective teams. It is between teams that seemed to use their challenges earlier and teams that were more willing to wait.

Among clubs with at least 40 challenges, the Twins used 46.1 percent of their challenges in innings one through three. The Angels were right behind them at 45.9 percent, with the White Sox at 45.5 percent. That is one kind of spring profile: get into the system early, use the resource, and do not wait for the late innings to start engaging it.

The Cubs looked different. Of their 70 spring challenges, 38.6 percent came in the seventh inning or later, the highest late-game share among teams with at least 40 challenges. The Diamondbacks were next at 36.4 percent, followed by the Cardinals at 34.4 percent. That is a different behavioral pattern. It suggests some clubs were already treating ABS less like a general correction tool and more like a resource they were willing to hold for later spots.

The Cardinals may have shown the clearest hybrid profile of the spring. They logged 61 challenges, posted a 65.6 percent overturn rate, and also led the 40-plus-challenge teams in late-and-close share at 16.4 percent. That does not prove they have solved challenge strategy. Spring is too noisy for that. But it does suggest that the first strong ABS identities were not purely about how often a team challenged. They were about when those challenges showed up.

## Timing Looked Different From Volume

This is the spring finding I would carry forward most aggressively into Opening Day: challenge timing already looks different from challenge volume.

Early in games, challenges converted at a much higher rate than they did late. Challenges in innings one through three were overturned 58.8 percent of the time. In innings four through six, that rate dropped to 52.8 percent. For challenges in the seventh inning or later, it dropped again. Late-but-not-close challenges were overturned at a 47.0 percent clip, and truly late-and-close challenges, seventh inning or later with the score within one run, were overturned only 40.3 percent of the time.

That does not mean late challenges are mistakes. It means they live in different conditions.

Late challenges carry more pressure. Teams are more likely to be spending a scarcer resource in a narrower game state, and the calls that get challenged there may be less obvious than the clean early-game misses that drove spring overturn totals. In other words, a lower raw overturn rate late does not automatically mean worse process. It may mean the system is being used in a more strategic environment.

That is why raw challenge totals are not enough. A team can challenge often without being especially sharp, and a team can challenge less often while still using the system in more consequential spots. Spring did not give us the final answer on that question, but it did make the question impossible to ignore.

## The Umpire Side Was Not Uniform

The umpire picture was just as uneven.

Jen Pawol and Mitch Trzeciak each saw 47 challenged calls this spring, the highest totals in the dataset. Pete Talkington saw 40. Matt Blackborow and Derek Thomas each saw 39. That is meaningful exposure over a relatively short spring.

The overturn rates attached to that exposure were not uniform. Among umpires with at least 25 challenged calls, Charlie Welling finished at 64.7 percent overturned, Matt Blackborow at 64.1 percent, Pete Talkington at 62.5 percent, and Jen Pawol at 61.7 percent. On the other end of the same sample floor, Macon Hammond finished at 36.0 percent, Marvin Hudson at 38.5 percent, and Thomas Fornarola at 41.2 percent.

That range is too wide to dismiss as simple noise, but spring is also too small to turn it into a final judgment on any individual umpire. The right takeaway is not that the spring produced permanent umpire truths. It is that ABS pressure was not evenly distributed and not every plate profile looked the same under review.

That matters for teams because ABS is not experienced in the abstract. It is experienced against specific umpire environments. If some plate profiles produce more challenge traffic, more overturned calls, or simply more uncertainty, teams and broadcasters will feel that long before the numbers stabilize over a full season.

## What Matters on Opening Day

The best way to carry the spring forward is not to hunt for one magic number. It is to watch for whether these early identities survive contact with the regular season.

Here is what I will be watching first:

- whether the high-volume clubs from spring stay high-volume once games start counting
- whether the late-game teams, especially clubs like the Cubs and Cardinals, keep holding challenges for the back third of games
- whether early-game heavy profiles like the Twins and Angels stay front-loaded or normalize
- whether the wide umpire exposure bands narrow once regular-season assignments stabilize
- whether late-and-close challenge success remains materially lower than the spring baseline

Spring did not solve ABS. It did something more useful. It gave the system its first real shape.

The spring dataset says ABS is already producing team style, umpire variance, and game-state tension that are worth tracking on their own terms. That is a stronger result than a simple overturn-rate recap. By Opening Day, the challenge system already looks like part of baseball's strategic surface, not just a rules experiment that happens to interrupt at-bats.
